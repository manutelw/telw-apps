Key Point 1 · Discussing purposes and reasons

Explain why clearly

toin order tofor -ingso thatbecause

Warm-up

Choose three objects or apps you use often. What do you use each one for, and why?

Listen · Natural conversation

Tara: Why do you back up your files?

Manav: I back them up so that I don't lose important work.

Tara: Where do you keep the copies?

Manav: I use cloud storage for keeping one copy, and an external drive for another.

Tara: Why two places?

Manav: Because one system can fail.

Tara: Makes sense. Do you organise the folders too?

Manav: Yes, in order to find things quickly when I need them.

Work it out

Which sentence expresses purpose correctly?

Language focus

Use to / in order to + verb for purpose, for + -ing for use, so that + clause for the result you want, and because + reason to explain why.

Rep 2 · For -ing

Rep 3 · So that

Explain one precaution you take and the result you want.

Rep 4 · Because

Give a reason for one digital habit.

Rep 5 · 30 seconds

Explain why you use three digital tools.

Key Point 2 · Understanding common IT terms

Make technical words simple

hardwareonlinebrowsersearch enginefilememory

Warm-up

Which computer words also have an ordinary everyday meaning outside IT?

Listen · Natural conversation

Zoya: My uncle says computer words are too technical.

Arun: Some are simpler than they sound. A file is just a stored document.

Zoya: And a browser?

Arun: It's the program you use to look around the web.

Zoya: What about hardware?

Arun: That's the physical equipment—the parts you can touch.

Zoya: So a search engine helps you find things online.

Arun: Exactly. If you can explain the job of the thing, the word becomes easier.

Work it out

What does hardware mean in IT?

Language focus

Many IT words become easier when you explain their function or connect them to an ordinary meaning: file, mouse, memory, web, browser. Use simple definitions: A browser is a program you use to open and move around websites.

Key Point 3 · Writing emails

Use spoken-friendly email language

good to hear from youthanks forattachedlet you knowlook forward to

Warm-up

What phrases make an email friendly but still professional?

Listen · Planning an email

Neel: I need to email a colleague about next week's visit.

Sara: Start by acknowledging them: 'Good to hear from you.'

Neel: Then I need to tell her the meeting time.

Sara: You can say, 'I just wanted to let you know the meeting is at three.'

Neel: And the map?

Sara: Say, 'I've attached the map.'

Neel: How should I finish?

Sara: Something like, 'Looking forward to seeing you.' Short and clear.

Work it out

Which line clearly states the purpose of the email?

Language focus

A clear friendly email usually moves through opening → purpose → details/action → close. Useful chunks include Good to hear from you, Thanks for letting me know, I've attached..., I just wanted to let you know..., and Looking forward to....

Rep 1 · Opening

Give a natural opening to a colleague you know.

Rep 2 · Purpose

Tell the reader why you are writing.

Rep 3 · Attachment

Mention a file you have attached and what it is for.

Rep 4 · Close

Close the message warmly without sounding casual.

Rep 5 · Whole email aloud

Speak a short complete email: opening, purpose, detail and close.
