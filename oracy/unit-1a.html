Greeting notes · When to use them
Hello

It doesn’t matter if you’re giving a president a handshake or buying a milkshake, this greeting is good for all situations, formal and informal.

Hi.

In the past, this was a more informal greeting. Now, it’s used much more generally — for example, in stores, for buying food and drink, and quite often in business. But in very formal situations, it’s better to use “Hello”.

Nice to meet you.

This is a very common greeting between people meeting for the first time. It’s fine for all situations, formal and informal.

Pleased to meet you

This is a slightly more formal greeting for meeting people for the first time.

How do you do?

This is a very formal greeting for first meetings. It’s less common nowadays. People learning English often reply “Fine, thanks” or “Very well, thanks”. But you should reply with a greeting. Say “How do you do?”, “Nice to meet you.” or “Pleased to meet you”.

How are you?

This can be formal or informal. We use it when we see people we know or have met before. Common replies are “Fine, thanks” and “Very well, thanks”. Sometimes, this greeting is used when people meet for the first time. In this case, repeat “How are you?” or say “Nice to meet you”.

Nice to see you. · Good to see you. · Great to see you

These greetings can be formal or informal. They’re normally used when you see someone you’ve met before, but haven’t seen for some time.

Listen · Before a workshop

Neha: Hi, I’m Neha. I don’t think we’ve spoken before.

Arjun: No, I don’t think we have. I’m Arjun. Nice to meet you.

Neha: Nice to meet you too. Are you here for the analytics workshop?

Arjun: Yes. I’m looking forward to it. How about you?

Neha: Same here. I’ve heard the speaker is very practical.

Arjun: That’s good to know. Is this your first session today?

Neha: It is. I just arrived a few minutes ago.

Arjun: Me too. Looks like we picked the right room.

What makes the opening work?

Language focus

A natural first contact usually has three small moves: greet → identify yourself → give the other person an easy way to respond.

Useful patterns: Hi, I’m … · I don’t think we’ve spoken before. · Are you here for …? · How are you finding …?

Rep 1 · Supported

You meet another learner before class. Greet them and introduce yourself.

Rep 2 · Add a question

Introduce yourself, then ask one easy question about the event or class.

Rep 3 · More formal

You are introduced to a visiting industry speaker. Open the conversation professionally.

Rep 4 · The other person is quiet

The person only says: “Hi.” Keep the opening alive without firing questions at them.

Rep 5 · No preparation

You enter a networking session and stand next to someone you do not know. Start naturally.

Listen · Coffee break

Vikram: I work in operations, but I’m studying data analytics this term.

Sana: Interesting. Are you using it at work already?

Vikram: A little. Our team usually tracks delivery issues manually, but this month we’re testing a new dashboard.

Sana: That sounds useful. What are you working on personally?

Vikram: I’m looking at delays in one region. I normally handle vendor coordination, so the data is helping me see patterns more clearly.

Sana: Nice. I work in marketing, and we’re doing something similar with campaign data right now.

Vikram: Really? What are you measuring?

Language weave

Use the present simple for a normal or regular situation: I work in finance. I travel twice a month.

Use the present continuous for something current or temporary: I’m working on a short project. We’re testing a new system this week.

The goal is not to recite grammar. The goal is to give the other person something they can respond to.

Rep 1 · One normal fact

Say what you normally do: study, work, travel, train, or manage.

Rep 2 · Add what is current

Say what you normally do, then add what you are doing at the moment.

Rep 3 · Return the conversation

Give your answer, then ask the other person a connected question.

Rep 4 · Handle a short reply

The other person says: “I’m working on a project.” Show interest and help them say more.

Rep 5 · 30-second exchange

Imagine you are at a coffee break. Explain what keeps you busy these days and invite the other person into the conversation.

Warm-up

What makes a first conversation easy? What makes it awkward? Think about phone calls, face-to-face meetings, online calls, noisy places, unfamiliar accents, and speaking to strangers.

Listen · Waiting for a guest lecture

Rhea: It’s easier to talk to people when there’s something around you to comment on.

Imran: Definitely. If I have to start from nothing, I sometimes overthink it.

Rhea: Same here. When I’m at an event, I usually ask about the session or the speaker.

Imran: That works. And if the other person gives a short answer, I try one follow-up instead of changing topics immediately.

Rhea: Exactly. You can also react first: “That sounds interesting,” and then ask more.

Imran: True. And when you need to leave, it helps to signal it politely instead of just disappearing.

Rhea: Yes. Something simple like, “I’ll let you get back to the group. Good talking to you.”

Imran: Right. A good ending makes the whole conversation feel better.

Work it out

What is the main idea?

Language weave

Use when for situations you expect or describe generally: When I meet someone new, I start with the situation around us.

Use if for a condition or possibility: If the other person gives a short answer, I try one follow-up.

Conversation pattern: react → connect → follow up → close when needed.

Rep 1 · React

The other person says: “I’m new here.” Give a natural reaction.

Rep 2 · React + follow up

They say: “I’m here for the finance talk.” React and ask one connected question.

Rep 3 · Use if/when naturally

Explain what you normally do when you meet someone new, and what you do if the conversation slows down.

Rep 4 · Exit politely

You need to rejoin your team. Close the conversation without sounding abrupt.

Rep 5 · Full first-contact performance

Imagine a professional event. Start with a stranger, exchange a little information, respond to what they say, ask a follow-up, and close naturally.
