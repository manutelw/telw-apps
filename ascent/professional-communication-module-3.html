Module 3

Raise a Deadline Problem Early

Tell a manager about a timing risk early, without sounding negative, defensive or as if you are passing the problem upwards.

1 · Train
2 · Practise
3 · Test
4 · Review

Train

A deadline problem is easier to manage before it becomes a missed deadline. State what is at risk, explain briefly, show what you are doing and agree the next move.

Show me

The deadline-risk framework

Do not hide the problem, but do not arrive with only bad news. Give the manager enough information to make a decision.

1. FlagWhat is at risk, and by when?
2. ExplainWhat is the brief reason?
3. RecoverWhat have I done, or what option do I have?
4. AgreeWhat next step, priority or support should we confirm?
Flag → Explain → Recover → Agree

What to notice

  • The risk appears in the first sentence, not at the end.
  • The reason is short. It explains; it does not defend.
  • The employee shows ownership before asking for help.
  • The final point gives the manager a decision or clear next step.

Keep the relationship right

  • Do not blame another person or team.
  • Do not over-apologise or sound panicked.
  • Do not promise a deadline you cannot realistically protect.
  • Give the manager time to plan around the risk.

Weak exchange

Manager: “How is the client report looking for four o’clock?”
Employee: “There might be a bit of a problem. I’ve been waiting on the data team, and they still haven’t sent everything. I’m trying, but it’s been difficult.”
Manager: “So are we going to miss four o’clock?”
Employee: “Possibly. I’ll do my best.”

Why this is weak: the manager has to drag out the actual risk. The explanation sounds defensive, ownership is unclear, and “I’ll do my best” gives no useful recovery plan.

Stronger exchange

Manager: “How is the client report looking for four o’clock?”
Employee: “We’re at risk of missing four o’clock because one set of figures is still outstanding. I’ve completed the sections that do not depend on it and followed up for the missing data. If it arrives by two, I can still finish today. If not, I’d like to agree whether we send the completed sections first or move the full report to tomorrow morning.”
Manager: “Send me the completed sections first. Keep me posted on the figures.”
Employee: “Understood. I’ll send the completed sections and update you as soon as the figures come in.”

See the framework inside the answer

Flag“We’re at risk of missing four o’clock...”
Explain“...because one set of figures is still outstanding.”
Recover“I’ve completed the sections that do not depend on it and followed up for the missing data.”
Agree“...agree whether we send the completed sections first or move the full report to tomorrow morning.”

Notice the follow-up: when the manager chooses an option, the employee closes the loop. There is no second explanation and no attempt to reopen the decision.

What changed?

Weak deadline warning

  • Hides the real risk
  • Explains before stating the problem
  • Sounds defensive
  • No usable recovery option

Stronger deadline warning

  • Flags the risk early
  • Keeps the reason brief
  • Shows ownership and recovery work
  • Ends with a decision or next step

Good escalation is early and useful. The point is not to hand the problem to the manager; it is to give the manager time and options.

Practise

The manager will speak twice. Raise the timing risk clearly, then respond to the follow-up.
