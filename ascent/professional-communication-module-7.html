Module 7

Give Constructive Feedback Without Making It Personal

When a colleague’s work is creating a problem, talk about the work, the effect and the next step — not the person.

1 · Train
2 · Practise
3 · Test
4 · Review

Train

Useful feedback is specific enough to act on. Describe what happened, explain the work impact, ask for a practical change and agree what happens next.

Show me

The constructive-feedback framework

Keep the conversation on observable work. Avoid labels such as “careless”, “unprofessional” or “you always”.

1. DescribeWhat specific behaviour or work output did I observe?
2. ImpactWhat effect did it have on the work?
3. AskWhat specific change would help, and is there a reason I should understand?
4. AgreeWhat will we do next?
Describe → Impact → Ask → Agree

What to notice

  • Describe one recent, observable example rather than judging the person.
  • Explain the impact on time, quality, customers or the team.
  • Ask for one clear change that the colleague can actually make.
  • Leave room for information you may not know.
  • Finish with an agreed next step.

Keep the relationship right

  • Do not use “always” or “never”.
  • Do not collect five old complaints into one conversation.
  • Do not soften the point so much that the colleague cannot tell what needs to change.
  • Do not attack motive, attitude or personality.

Weak exchange

Employee: “You keep making these spreadsheets difficult to use. You really need to be more careful.”
Colleague: “What do you mean?”
Employee: “Every time I get your files, I have to fix them.”
Colleague: “I didn’t know there was a problem.”

Why this is weak: “you keep” and “be more careful” judge the person but do not identify one usable change. The colleague is likely to defend themselves rather than solve the work problem.

Stronger exchange

Employee: “Could I flag one thing about the weekly sheet? The last two files used a different column order from the standard template, so I had to rearrange them before combining the data. Could we use the standard order from next week? Is there anything in the template that is making that difficult?”
Colleague: “I was using an older version of the template. I didn’t realise it had changed.”
Employee: “That explains it. I’ll send you the current version now, and let’s use that one from next week.”
Colleague: “Yes, that works.”

See the framework inside the answer

Describe“The last two files used a different column order from the standard template.”
Impact“...so I had to rearrange them before combining the data.”
Ask“Could we use the standard order from next week? Is there anything in the template that is making that difficult?”
Agree“I’ll send you the current version now, and let’s use that one from next week.”

Notice the shift: the stronger version is clear without being personal. It also discovers useful information: the colleague had an old template.

What changed?

Weak feedback

  • Judges the person
  • Uses a broad pattern claim
  • Gives no precise action
  • Creates defensiveness

Stronger feedback

  • Names one observable example
  • Explains the work impact
  • Requests a specific change
  • Invites useful context and agrees the next step

Good feedback protects the standard without turning the conversation into a judgement of the person.

Practise

Give a colleague one clear piece of feedback. Keep it factual, useful and easy to act on.
